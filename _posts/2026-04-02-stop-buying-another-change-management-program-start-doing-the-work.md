There's a thriving industry built on a very seductive promise: buy this program, earn this certification, follow this framework — and you'll know how to lead change. Tens of thousands of change managers across the world have taken the bait. And to be fair, there's value in structured learning. But here's the uncomfortable truth that the industry doesn't advertise: no program can teach you what the work itself will.

Change management, done well, is not a formula. It is a craft. And crafts are learned by doing them — messily, imperfectly, and repeatedly.

## The Program Trap

Walk into any change management conference and you'll see the landscape clearly. ADKAR. Kotter. Prosci. Lewin. McKinsey's 7-S. Each has its advocates, its certification pathway, its community of practitioners who will defend it as the right way.

And the programs keep coming. Newer, shinier, more expansive. Some are genuinely good. Many are repackaged versions of ideas that have been around for decades, dressed up in fresh language and sold at premium prices to organisations desperately searching for certainty in uncertain times.

Here's what they all have in common: they're models. And models are, by definition, simplifications of reality.

Reality doesn't simplify. Real change happens inside real organisations, with real people who have real fears, real histories, real politics, and real competing priorities. No framework accounts for the team that was restructured twice in eighteen months and is now quietly resistant to anything that looks like another initiative. No certification prepares you for the executive sponsor who says all the right things in the steering committee and then completely undermines the project in the hallway. You learn to navigate those situations by navigating them — not by studying a slide deck about them.

## What Experience Actually Teaches You

In over three decades of change work, the most important lessons I've learned didn't come from a program. They came from:

The project that failed spectacularly — and forced me to understand why people resist change at a human level, not just a process level.

The leader who was technically brilliant and interpersonally catastrophic — and taught me that stakeholder engagement is not a communication plan, it's a relationship.

The initiative that was perfectly designed and badly timed — and showed me that context is everything, and that even good change can land badly when the organisation isn't ready for it.

You cannot buy that kind of education. You accumulate it.

## The Danger of Framework Dependency

There's a subtler problem with over-relying on programs and frameworks: they can become a crutch that actually reduces your effectiveness.

When you're highly trained in a specific methodology, there's a temptation to fit every situation into that methodology's lens — even when it doesn't quite fit. The framework becomes the answer before you've properly understood the question.

Real change leadership requires the confidence to sit with ambiguity. To assess a situation without reaching for a pre-packaged response. To trust your judgment, your read of the room, your understanding of the people involved — and to adapt in real time as things evolve. That confidence doesn't come from completing a course. It comes from having done the work, made the call, lived with the consequences, and come back wiser.

## So What Should You Do Instead?

To be clear: learning matters. Reading matters. Structured thinking helps. If you're new to change management, foundational frameworks give you a useful starting map — just don't mistake the map for the territory.

But if you want to actually develop as a change practitioner, here's what will move the needle:

Take on real change work, even when you don't feel ready. The discomfort is where the development happens.

Reflect deliberately after every project — what worked, what didn't, and more importantly, why. This is where the real lessons live.

Find experienced practitioners who can give you honest feedback, not just mentors who validate what you're already doing.

Stop waiting for the right program to make you ready. You're already closer than you think.

## The Bottom Line

The change management industry will keep selling programs. That's fine — some of them are worth your time and money. But they are inputs, not outputs. They inform the work. They don't replace it.

The best change managers I've encountered over three decades share one common trait. Not the same certifications. Not the same methodology. They share this: they got in and did the work — early, often, and with a willingness to learn from what went wrong as much as what went right.

That's the program that actually works. And you can start today.
