Every change initiative encounters friction. That's not a problem — it's a given. The mistake most organisations make isn't that they experience friction, it's that they fail to diagnose what kind of friction they're actually dealing with. Resistance and sabotage are not the same thing. Treating them as if they are is one of the most costly errors a change leader can make — and it's far more common than anyone in the room usually wants to admit.

## Resistance Is Normal. It's Also Useful.

Let's start by rehabilitating resistance's reputation, because it's taken a battering it doesn't entirely deserve.

When people resist change, they are usually doing something entirely human. They're expressing uncertainty. They're protecting something that matters to them — their routine, their identity, their sense of competence, their team. They might be voicing a concern nobody else has had the courage to raise.

Resistance is visible. It's often vocal. And critically, it's workable. A resistant person hasn't made up their mind. They're in the conversation, even if they're not happy about it. With the right engagement — genuine listening, honest dialogue, time to process — resistant people frequently become some of the most committed advocates for the change.

The signal resistance sends is: something needs more attention here. That's useful information. Dismissing it as a people problem to be managed misses the point entirely.

## Sabotage Is Something Else.

Sabotage is deliberate. It's not someone struggling with uncertainty — it's someone who has decided, consciously or otherwise, to work against the change. And unlike resistance, it often doesn't announce itself.

Saboteurs don't raise their hand in the town hall. They agree in the room and undermine outside of it. They selectively filter information to their teams. They create quiet coalitions of opposition. They delay, deflect, and reframe — not because they're confused, but because they've chosen a side.

The damage sabotage does is compounded by how long it goes unaddressed. Because it's covert, it can be rationalised away for months. Leaders convince themselves the initiative just needs better communication, or more time, or stronger sponsorship. Sometimes that's true. But sometimes the communication is landing just fine — and certain people are working hard to make sure it doesn't stick.

## It Happens at Every Level

Here's the part nobody puts in the change management textbooks: sabotage doesn't only come from the shop floor. It comes from the top.

Senior leaders who publicly champion a change initiative while privately signalling to their direct reports that it won't last. Middle managers who translate organisational messages with just enough cynicism to kill momentum in the room. High-influence individuals — often long-tenured, well-respected — who have decided the change threatens something they value and use their social capital to erode confidence in it.

The seniority of the saboteur doesn't make it more acceptable. It makes it more damaging. Because the higher up it sits, the more it poisons the well for everyone below.

## So What Do You Do?

First: learn to tell them apart. Resistance tends to be emotional and expressed. It shows up in conversations, in feedback, in body language. It's a response to what the change feels like. Sabotage tends to be behavioural and strategic. It shows up in patterns — the missed milestones that keep having plausible explanations, the team that always seems to be behind, the messages that somehow never quite reach the people they were meant to reach.

When you sense something is off, ask yourself honestly: is this person struggling with the change, or are they working against it? The answer changes everything about how you respond.

Second: hold yourself accountable for naming it. This is where many leaders — and many change practitioners — fall short. Calling out resistance is relatively easy. Calling out sabotage means having a harder conversation, usually with someone who has authority or influence. It means naming behaviour that's been politely ignored. It means accepting that not everyone who nods in the boardroom is actually on board.

That discomfort is not a reason to avoid the conversation. It's the whole point of having it. A change initiative that's being actively undermined will not succeed no matter how good the strategy is. Accountability requires you to see what's actually happening — and to respond to it accordingly.

Third: don't lose sight of the people who are genuinely resistant. While you're addressing the sabotage, don't conflate it with the resistance. The people who are openly struggling with the change are not your adversaries. They're your opportunity. Meet them where they are. Understand what they're protecting. Bring them into the solution.

The change leader who can distinguish between someone who's worried and someone who's working against the change — and respond to both with clarity and integrity — is the kind of leader change actually needs.

## The Bottom Line

Resistance is a signal. Sabotage is a choice. Both require a response — but not the same one.

The accountability gap in change management isn't just about holding others to account. It's about being honest enough with yourself to see what's in front of you, name it accurately, and act on it. That takes courage, experience, and a willingness to have uncomfortable conversations.

Those qualities, unfortunately, don't come in a program. But these qualities can sometimes be what separates the change leaders who get results from the ones who wonder why nothing ever seems to land.

A tough lesson I've learned (and continue to learn) and sometimes it has not been in a very graceful way!
